# Discuss and Document Off-Label Use of Weight-Loss Medications

## STEP 1 — Gather Information
- The drug proposed and its regulatory status for weight loss: not FDA approved for this indication, or approved but not for chronic administration (e.g., phentermine, which "is not approved for long-term use").
- Drug classes the guideline lists as used off-label for weight loss: topiramate, zonisamide, metformin, GLP-1 agonists such as exenatide and liraglutide, bupropion, ADHD drugs such as methylphenidate, thyroid hormones, and combinations of these.
- Prescriber setting: research context, and whether the prescriber has expertise in weight management or endocrinology.
- Whether the patient has been informed about FDA-approved weight-loss medications.
- For long-term phentermine: cardiovascular history, psychiatric history, substance-abuse history, pulse and BP on treatment, weight response.

## STEP 2 — Rule In / Rule Out
- Rec 3.1: "We suggest against the off-label use of medications approved for other disease states for the sole purpose of producing weight loss. A trial of such therapy can be attempted in the context of research and by healthcare providers with expertise in weight management dealing with a well-informed patient." (Ungraded Best Practice Recommendation)
- Rule out: prescriber without weight-management or endocrinology expertise; the guideline states such physicians "are advised against prescribing off-label medications."
- Rule out: a patient who is not well informed.

## STEP 3 — Classify or Stratify
- Category A: drug not FDA approved for weight loss (off-label indication) -> apply the minimum counselling and documentation in STEP 4.
- Category B: phentermine prescribed long term (approved for weight loss, not for long-term use) -> the guideline considers it reasonable only if ALL five apply:
  1. No evidence of serious cardiovascular disease.
  2. No serious psychiatric disease or history of substance abuse.
  3. Informed about FDA-approved long-term weight-loss medications, and told these have been documented to be safe and effective whereas phentermine has not.
  4. No clinically significant increase in pulse or BP while taking phentermine.
  5. Significant weight loss while using the medication.
- State Medical Board direction and local laws always take precedence.

## STEP 4 — Decide
If proceeding, at minimum:
- Advise the patient that the approach "has not been evaluated for safety and efficacy and is not approved by the FDA."
- Document that discussion, plus the risks and benefits presented to the patient, in the medical record.
- Discuss FDA-approved weight-loss medications and document why the off-label medication was chosen over one of these.
- For long-term phentermine: document the five criteria in the record, and document the off-label nature of the prescribing at each visit.
- Phentermine dosing per the guideline: start 7.5 or 15 mg/d; increase only if clinically significant weight loss is not achieved.
- Phentermine follow-up: at least monthly during dose escalation, then at least every 3 months on a stable dose.

## Clinical Guardrails / Mimics / Pitfalls
- Recommendation 3.1 is ungraded best-practice advice; the default is against off-label prescribing for weight loss alone.
- Do not sell weight-loss medications from the office; this "could be interpreted as representing a conflict of interest."
- Phentermine has no long-term safety or efficacy data; the guideline cites low addiction potential in 269 long-term patients, but that does not replace the documentation steps.
- Intermittent phentermine therapy is described as "not a logical way to prescribe" given how weight-loss drugs affect weight regulation.
- Combinations of the listed drugs are also off-label use.
- Do not let the counselling and documentation lapse at follow-up; the off-label nature must be documented at every visit.

## Concrete Clinical Example
An adult with obesity has done well on short-term phentermine, and the clinician, who has weight-management expertise, considers continuing it long term. The patient has no serious cardiovascular or psychiatric disease and no substance-abuse history. Pulse and BP have not risen significantly, and weight loss has been significant. The clinician explains that long-term use is off-label, not evaluated for safety and efficacy, and not FDA approved. They review the FDA-approved long-term options and record why phentermine was chosen. The note lists all five criteria and the risks and benefits discussed. The dose stays at 15 mg/d, follow-up is at least every 3 months, and every visit note states that the prescribing is off-label.

**Source:** Pharmacological Management of Obesity: An Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline, Endocrine Society, 2015, DOI:10.1210/jc.2014-3415
