# Teach the Self-Administration Decision: Oral Stress Dose vs Emergency Parenteral Hydrocortisone

## STEP 1 — Gather Information
- Confirm primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI) on glucocorticoid replacement; note usual oral dose, fludrocortisone, age (adult, adolescent, school-age child, infant) and who will inject (patient, partner, parent).
- Ask about prior adrenal crises, past injection teaching, and whether the patient has ever self-injected (in one survey only 12% of patients with a crisis in the previous year injected themselves).
- Check the patient holds an injection kit (vials of 100 mg hydrocortisone sodium, syringes, needles) and a leaflet for health care staff; kit issuance and card details belong to sibling skills.

## STEP 2 — Rule In / Rule Out
- Rule in: every PAI patient. Rec 4.4: "We suggest patient education concerning glucocorticoid adjustments in stressful events and adrenal crisis-prevention strategies including parenteral self- or lay-administration of emergency glucocorticoids." (Ungraded best practice statement)
- Rule in: Rec 4.6: "every patient should be equipped with a glucocorticoid injection kit for emergency use and be educated on how to use it." (Ungraded best practice statement)
- Rule out using this skill as treatment: a patient already in suspected adrenal crisis needs Rec 4.1 hospital management now, not teaching.

## STEP 3 — Classify or Stratify
Teach the patient to sort each situation into one of two tiers (Table 4 sick day rules):
- **Oral tier (Sick day rule 1)**: double the routine oral dose for fever or illness requiring bed rest, when requiring antibiotics for an infection, or before a small outpatient procedure (eg, dental work). Home fever plan (Table 3): double (>38°C) or triple (>39°C) until recovery, usually 2 to 3 d, with electrolyte-containing fluids as tolerated.
- **Inject tier (Sick day rule 2)**: inject a glucocorticoid preparation im or iv in case of severe illness, trauma, persistent vomiting, when fasting for a procedure (colonoscopy), or during surgical intervention. If unable to tolerate oral medication due to vomiting or trauma, "early parenteral (iv, im, or sc) injection of 100 mg hydrocortisone is indicated."
- Symptoms and signs of emergent crisis also move the patient to the inject tier; major features are hypotension and volume depletion. In pediatric CAH data the median time from first symptoms to overt crisis was 1 day, so teach not to wait.

## STEP 4 — Decide
1. Dose to self-inject (Table 3, unable to tolerate oral medication): adults im or sc hydrocortisone 100 mg; children im 50 mg/m2 or estimate: infants 25 mg, school-age children 50 mg, adolescents 100 mg.
2. Route: im is one injection and reached cortisol >1000 nmol/L (>36 µg/dL) faster (11 vs 22 min); sc needed two injections but was the route patients preferred, and its delay compares favorably with waiting for health professionals. Let the patient choose a route they will actually use.
3. Alternative only where available: rectal prednisolone 100 mg suppository; not with diarrhea, not widely available (eg, not in the United States).
4. After any emergency injection: go to hospital ("Enforcing the need to go to hospital after emergency injection"); show the leaflet advising 100 mg hydrocortisone immediately iv or im, followed by continuous infusion of 200 mg/24 h.
5. Give the emergency phone number of the endocrine specialist team.
6. Reinforce and confirm understanding at each follow-up visit (at least annually if no specific problems or recent crises; otherwise more frequently). Rec 5.4 (Ungraded best practice statement) adds identification of precipitating symptoms and signs and how to act in impending adrenal crisis.

## Clinical Guardrails / Mimics / Pitfalls
- Persistent vomiting means oral doubling will not work; teach that this is an injection trigger, not a reason to retry tablets.
- Gastrointestinal disease (32.6%) and other infections (24.3%) were the main crisis precipitants; respiratory infections dominate in early childhood, gastrointestinal at older ages.
- Training in self-injection is likely to lower the threshold for parenteral use; 46% of patients were not sufficiently skilled in stress management, so repeat teaching rather than assume retention.
- About one-third of emergencies occur outside the home: the kit must travel with the patient.
- Rationale for erring high: the guideline places a higher value on preventing adrenal crisis than on short-term overtreatment.
- Mineralocorticoid replacement is not required if the hydrocortisone dose exceeds 50 mg/24 hours.

## Concrete Clinical Example
A 28-year-old man with autoimmune PAI calls with gastroenteritis. Last night he had fever 38.4°C and correctly doubled his hydrocortisone. This morning he has vomited three times, including his tablets, and feels lightheaded on standing. Using sick day rule 2, he moves to the inject tier and gives himself hydrocortisone 100 mg im from his kit (he chose im after learning it is a single injection). He then goes to the emergency department, shows his leaflet, and receives continuous hydrocortisone 200 mg/24 h. At follow-up the clinic re-checks his technique and confirms he knows the triggers for each tier.
