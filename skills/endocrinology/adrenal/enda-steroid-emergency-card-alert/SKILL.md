# Equip Every PAI Patient With a Steroid Emergency Card and Medical Alert Identification

## STEP 1 — Gather Information
- Confirm the diagnosis of primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI) and the current glucocorticoid (and mineralocorticoid) regimen.
- Ask whether the patient already holds a steroid emergency card and whether it is available and up to date.
- Ask whether the patient wears medical alert identification (bracelet or necklace).
- Note any prior adrenal crisis or encounters where emergency staff were unaware of the need for immediate treatment.

## STEP 2 — Rule In / Rule Out
- Rule in: all patients with PAI. Recommendation 4.5 applies to "all patients", regardless of disease duration or crisis history.
- Nothing rules a PAI patient out of this recommendation.

## STEP 3 — Classify or Stratify
- No card, or card lost: issue a card now.
- Card present but not up to date: reissue or update it.
- Card present and current, no medical alert identification: advise a bracelet or necklace.
- Card and identification both in place: reconfirm at each review.

## STEP 4 — Decide
Guideline recommendation 4.5 (Ungraded best practice statement), quoted verbatim:
> "We recommend that all patients should be equipped with a steroid emergency card and medical alert identification to inform health personnel of the need for increased glucocorticoid doses to avert or treat adrenal crisis and the need of immediate parenteral steroid treatment in the event of an emergency."

Action points (Table 4, Measures for Prevention of Adrenal Crisis):
- Identify and define the problem — steroid emergency card: check that the card is available and up to date.
- Identify and define the problem — medical alert bracelet or necklace worded: "Adrenal insufficiency – needs steroids!"
- Provide patient with a leaflet with information on adrenal crisis and hospitalization to show to health care staff, clearly advising the need to inject 100 mg hydrocortisone immediately iv or im, followed by continuous infusion of 200 mg/24 h.
- Evidence text: cards should be issued to all patients to inform health care providers about the diagnosis of PAI and how to provide treatment for an adrenal crisis. Cards are issued by endocrinology departments, patient organizations, and the pharmaceutical industry; some European cards carry the national language on one side and English on the other.

## Clinical Guardrails / Mimics / Pitfalls
- The card supplements, and does not replace, the emergency injection kit and self-injection training (recommendation 4.6; see enda-glucocorticoid-injection-kit-training) and stress-dose education (4.4; see enda-patient-education-stress-dosing-self-admin).
- Rationale per guideline: many patients meet physicians unaware of the need for immediate treatment of an adrenal crisis; a steroid card can help overcome this.
- Check that the card is available and up to date (Table 4); reinforce education at each follow-up visit (Table 4, Follow-up).
- Treatment of an actual crisis is covered in enda-emergency-adrenal-crisis-treatment.

## Concrete Clinical Example
A patient with newly diagnosed autoimmune PAI is being discharged on oral hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone. Before discharge, the endocrinologist issues a steroid emergency card that informs health care providers of the PAI diagnosis and how to treat an adrenal crisis. The patient is advised to wear a medical alert bracelet reading "Adrenal insufficiency – needs steroids!" and is given a leaflet for emergency staff stating the need for 100 mg hydrocortisone iv or im immediately, followed by 200 mg/24 h by continuous infusion. At each follow-up visit, the team checks that the card is still available and up to date.
